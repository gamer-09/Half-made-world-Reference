# Chapter 0 — The Last Door

They had been running so long the boy no longer remembered the sound of his own name.

It came back to him sometimes, at the edges of sleep — a word his mother said when the fire was low and the world still had walls. Now there were no walls. There was only the dark between the trees, and the weight of his mother's hand on the back of his neck, and the sound behind them that never stopped.

The king's men did not shout when they came. That was the worst of it. A hunting party that screamed was a hunting party that wanted to scare you, and scared things made mistakes. These men made no sound at all. You heard them the way you heard a fever — not at first, and then everywhere. A boot turning on wet leaves. A horse breathing in the dark. The soft click of a bowstring, tested, not yet loosed.

The boy had asked once, in the first days, why the men were following. His mother had not answered. She had only pressed her hand flat against the center of his chest, over his heart, and he had felt it — a faint warmth, like the last coal of a dying fire — and then she had pulled him on. After that he stopped asking.

He understood now, in the way children understand the shape of a thing without knowing its name: they were the last. And the men with the quiet boots were coming to finish it.

---

They had gathered what was left of the people into a hollow at the foot of the mountains, three dozen of them, maybe fewer. The boy counted the torches the way his mother had taught him, and he knew before she did that one was missing.

The elders spoke in a language the boy was only half old enough to know. But he knew the tone of it. He had heard it in his mother's voice when she looked at him and did not say the thing she was thinking. It was the sound people made when they had decided something terrible and were telling each other it was the only way.

His mother knelt in front of him and took his face in her hands. Her skin was pale as ash, grey as the moon through smoke, and in the hollow of her throat a faint light moved under the skin, like something breathing. His own hands were the same. All their hands were the same. That was the reason for the torches, and the quiet boots, and the fever-sound that never stopped.

"Whatever you hear," she said, "do not let go of my hand."

He nodded.

"And if I fall," she said, "run toward the light. Only the light. Do not look back at me. Do you understand?"

He nodded again, though he did not understand at all, because the light had gone out of the world a long time ago.

---

The elders began to sing.

It was not a song the boy had heard at firesides. It was older, and it hurt to hear — a sound dragged up from the bottom of something, a note that made the air go thin and the trees lean toward them as if listening. The men with the torches stopped. For the first time in all the days of running, the boy heard them make a sound: a low, ragged breath, all at once, like men who had found what they were hunting and suddenly wished they had not.

The singing rose. The ground under the boy's feet turned soft and warm, the way it did in the dreamless dark of winter, and then it was not soft at all. It was splitting.

A seam of light tore open the air in front of the elders, and the boy saw — for one terrible, holy moment — the other side of it. A country with no sun. A dark that was not empty but *full*, moving with shapes that had not yet decided what they were.

His mother's grip closed on his hand. "Now."

And the world broke.

It did not break the way the boy had imagined things broke — a window, a bone. It broke the way a held breath breaks. Everything leaned. The torches went sideways. One of the king's men screamed, and the scream bent, and then the light swallowed it.

The boy ran toward the seam, dragged by his mother's hand, and he did not look back, because she had told him not to, and because looking back had never once made anything better.

---

On the other side, the air was wrong.

It did not smell of pine or rain or anything the boy knew. It smelled of stone that had never seen the sky. He stood there, panting, and the world behind him — the world with the trees and the fever-sound and the men — was gone, closed over like a wound that had healed wrong.

All around them, in the dark, something was already beginning to happen. The boy could feel it more than see it: a gathering, a thickening, like frost forming on glass. The elders had called this place a refuge. Looking at it now, the boy understood — with the clean, terrible clarity only children have — that they had been wrong.

The refuge was already hungry.

The first of the creatures came out of the dark within the hour. It had too many legs and not enough eyes, and it moved the way the shapes in the seam had moved, half-decided, unfinished. His mother put herself between it and the boy. Others did the same. The singing started again, weaker this time, and did not stop the creature. Nothing stopped the creatures. There were so many of them, and they were so new, and the people were so tired.

The boy learned, in that first night, that being the last of a people does not make you safe. It only means there is no one left to hide behind for very long.

---

By morning, or what passed for morning in a country with no sun, the boy was the only one left.

He did not know how it had happened. He had run, as his mother had told him to, toward what light there was — a faint glow at the heart of that hungry dark — and when he stopped running and turned around, there was no one behind him. Only the creatures, settling, and the quiet, and the last of the singing fading into the stone.

He was nine years old. He did not cry, because crying would have been a sound, and sound, in that place, was an invitation.

He stood in the dark of the realm his people had torn open to save themselves, and he understood three things, one after the other, like stones dropping into a well.

He was the last.

The refuge was a tomb.

And somewhere far behind him, on the other side of the closed door, a king was still waiting — a king who did not know that the thing he had been so afraid of had already died, and that the only thing left of it was a boy, alone in the dark, learning not to make a sound.

---

The boy who walked out of that place, years later, would take the name Kael von Ashen.

But that is another chapter. This one ends where every story truly begins:

with a child, in the dark, refusing to be the last.
